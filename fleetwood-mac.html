Fleetwood Mac

Fleetwood Mac is one of the most commercially successful and internally turbulent bands in rock history — a group whose identity has shifted so dramatically across decades that it almost constitutes several distinct bands sharing a name and a rhythm section. At their core, Mick Fleetwood and John McVie provide the one constant thread from the British blues outfit of 1967 through the soft-rock colossus of the Rumours era and beyond. Everything else — personnel, sound, geography, and creative gravity — has been negotiable.

The band’s story is best understood as a series of distinct chapters, each with its own internal logic and often its own critical reputation. The Peter Green years produced some of the most emotionally raw British blues ever recorded, and Green himself is now widely regarded as one of the greatest guitarists of his generation. The transitional early-1970s period, marked by revolving guitarists and Christine McVie’s growing compositional role, is frequently overlooked but is essential for understanding the conditions that made the Buckingham-Nicks addition so transformative. The 1975–1987 commercial peak — the period most listeners think of when they hear the name — is a story of extraordinary creative productivity conducted against a backdrop of collapsing personal relationships. And the post-classic era from the late 1980s onward is a case study in how legacy acts negotiate identity, reunion economics, and the competing claims of former members.

The notes below partition the band’s history and music into thematic arcs. They are meant to be read in any order, but the sequence below moves from foundations outward to context and legacy.
