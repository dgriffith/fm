Solo Careers and the Centrifugal Force Problem

One of the defining structural tensions in Fleetwood Mac’s classic-era history is that its most prominent members — Buckingham, Nicks, and to a lesser extent Christine McVie — all had solo careers that were commercially and artistically serious enough to constitute real alternatives to the band. This created a centrifugal pressure that the Mick Fleetwood/John McVie center had to perpetually resist.

Stevie Nicks’s Solo Career

Nicks’s solo trajectory is the most commercially impressive. Bella Donna (1981) debuted at number one; “Edge of Seventeen,” “Stand Back,” and “Talk to Me” are among the defining rock radio tracks of the decade. Her solo albums sold in the tens of millions and earned her a reputation as a separate star rather than merely a component of Fleetwood Mac — a reputation that shaped how she negotiated her position within the band. The tension between Nicks-as-solo-artist and Nicks-as-band-member is never far from the surface in interviews from the 1982–1990 period, and it shaped the band’s recording schedules (often arranged around her tour commitments) in ways that frustrated Buckingham. See Stevie Nicks — Myth, Persona, and Craft.

Lindsey Buckingham’s Solo Career

Buckingham’s solo work — Law and Order (1981), Go Insane (1984), Out of the Cradle (1992), and later releases — has a smaller audience than Nicks’s but is more artistically consistent with his avant-pop production instincts. “Trouble” from Law and Order is the commercial high point; the rest of the catalogue is idiosyncratic, technically sophisticated, and aimed at a listener who wants something more demanding than Fleetwood Mac at its most radio-ready. His solo career is probably best understood as the outlet for the Tusk impulse that the band kept frustrating.

Christine McVie’s Relative Restraint

Christine McVie released one solo album, Christine McVie (1984), and largely declined the solo-career arms race, which may explain why she is the member most consistently described by colleagues as a stabilizing presence. Her departure from the band in 1998 citing travel anxiety, and her return in 2014, is one of the more unusual career moves in classic rock — a genuine step away from fame on personal grounds, not a commercial calculation. See Christine McVie — The Underrated Architect.
