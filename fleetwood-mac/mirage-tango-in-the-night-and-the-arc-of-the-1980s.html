Mirage, Tango in the Night, and the arc of the 1980s

The 1980s represent Fleetwood Mac’s most commercially consistent decade and, for many listeners, their most aesthetically compromised one. Mirage (1982) was a deliberate retreat from the experiments of Tusk toward radio-friendly AOR; Tango in the Night (1987) was a career-peak commercial success achieved through studio technology that was cutting-edge at the time and has aged in complicated ways. Between these two albums, the band’s internal dynamics were shifting in ways that would eventually make the 1987 lineup the last stable one for decades.

Mirage (1982)

Mirage is the least discussed major-period Fleetwood Mac album, which is fair — it is the most calculated of the run. Buckingham’s contributions (“Oh Diane,” “Empire State”) are polished but lack the restless energy of his best work. Christine McVie delivers the album’s standout with “Hold Me,” a collaborative track with Buckingham that became one of the band’s biggest US singles. Stevie Nicks, whose solo career was peaking with Bella Donna and The Wild Heart, seems partially absent in spirit even when present in body. The album sold well and produced no interesting controversies, which is itself a kind of verdict.

Tango in the Night (1987)

Tango is a more genuinely strange record than it appears on the surface. Buckingham’s production embraces synthesizers, gated reverb, and a kind of crystalline digital sheen that was the sonic signature of mid-1980s pop. “Big Love,” with its acoustic guitar overdubbed into a dense polyrhythmic weave, is a technically remarkable piece of studio construction. “Little Lies” and “Everywhere” (both Christine McVie) are among the most purely enjoyable songs in the catalogue. “Seven Wonders” (Nicks) holds the Nicks end solidly.

The album was largely recorded with Buckingham and Christine McVie working in Buckingham’s home studio, with Nicks recording her vocals separately and the full band rarely in the same room. This distributed process anticipated the way many records are made today but felt at the time like a symptom of fragmentation. Buckingham’s departure immediately after the album’s release — refusing to tour behind its success — effectively ended the classic lineup’s first run.
