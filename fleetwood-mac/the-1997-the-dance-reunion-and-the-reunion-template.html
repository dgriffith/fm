The 1997 The Dance Reunion and the Reunion Template

The Dance (1997) — the MTV-produced reunion concert featuring the classic Rumours-era lineup (Buckingham, Nicks, Christine McVie, John McVie, Mick Fleetwood) for the first time since 1987 — is one of the most commercially successful live albums in rock history and established a template for how the band would manage its legacy going forward. Understanding what The Dance was designed to do clarifies the logic of subsequent lineup decisions.

The Reunion as Product

The reunion was brokered by MTV as part of its “Unplugged”-adjacent live concert series, though unlike Unplugged it was a full electric performance. The commercial logic was mutual: MTV got the highest-profile reunion of the decade; the band got a platform to reactivate its commercial presence after nearly a decade of diminished output and profile. The resulting album debuted at number one, sold over 5 million copies, and the accompanying tour was the highest-grossing of 1997.

Critically, the reunion was not a creative project — there were no new Buckingham-Nicks-McVie compositions, no attempt to extend the classic-era sound into new territory. It was explicitly retrospective, a performance of the catalogue for an audience that had grown up with it. This framing — the band as custodians of its own legacy rather than as ongoing creative entity — defines the post-1997 mode of operation.

Buckingham’s Place in the Template

The reunion depended on Buckingham’s participation. His role in the band is not just musical but psychological: he is the member who resists the legacy-act framing most actively, who insists on creative evolution, and whose presence signals (to audiences and to the band itself) that the reunion is not merely a nostalgia exercise. His absence from the 2018–2020 tour made that tour’s commercial success with Neil Finn and Mike Campbell a notable data point — the machine can run without him — but also changed what the tour meant. See Buckingham's Firing in 2018 and the Final Chapter Debate.

Lessons for Legacy Acts

The The Dance model — reunion marketed explicitly as retrospective, live album, massive tour, no creative ambitions beyond the catalogue — became a template for other legacy rock reunions. The commercial lesson (that audiences for classic rock will pay significant sums to see the familiar lineup perform the familiar songs) was well-learned across the industry. The artistic cost — the subordination of ongoing creative activity to the demands of the legacy — is what Buckingham argued against throughout his post-1997 tenure in the band, with limited success.
