Tusk — The Difficult Second Classic

Tusk (1979) is the most critically undervalued album in the Fleetwood Mac catalogue and the clearest evidence that Buckingham’s artistic ambitions were fundamentally incompatible with the band’s commercial apparatus. Released as a double album at a time when Rumours had spent more than a year on the charts, Tusk was a deliberate act of creative disruption — Buckingham’s response to the success of Rumours was, essentially, to refuse to make Rumours again.

What Buckingham Was Reacting To

The punk and new wave movements of 1977–1978 had exposed the self-satisfaction of arena rock, and Buckingham was paying attention in a way that the rest of the band largely was not. His contributions to Tusk — the title track (recorded with the USC Trojan Marching Band in a studio corridor), “Not That Funny,” “What Makes You Think You’re the One” — are sparse, rhythmically off-kilter, and deliberately unpolished. The title track in particular sounds like nothing else in the catalogue: a two-chord vamp with a marching band, no chorus, and a guitar tone closer to the Talking Heads than to the warm FM production of Rumours.

The Album as Ensemble Document

What’s frequently missed in the Buckingham-centric reading of Tusk is that Nicks and Christine McVie’s contributions are excellent and are largely continuous with the Rumours aesthetic. “Sara” is one of Nicks’s finest compositions — a long, elliptical meditation on a miscarriage (reportedly), creative identity, and romantic longing, built on a hypnotic piano loop. “Think About Me” and “Over My Head” (Christine) demonstrate that her pop instincts had sharpened, not softened. The album’s incoherence — if it is incoherent — comes from the gap between Buckingham’s post-punk experiments and the other two songwriters’ continued occupation of the Rumours emotional territory.

Critical and Commercial Reception

Tusk sold approximately 4 million copies — commercially disappointing by Rumours standards, critically respected in retrospect. The rehabilitation has been substantial: by the 2010s it was regularly appearing in “albums ahead of their time” lists, and Buckingham’s performance of the material on subsequent tours confirmed that the songs work live in ways the original recordings’ abrasiveness had obscured. The debate about whether Tusk or Rumours better represents the band’s peak is, in a sense, a debate about what the band fundamentally was.
