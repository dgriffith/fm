My Notes

The Buckingham-Nicks Recruitment and What It Changed

The story of how Lindsey Buckingham and Stevie Nicks came to join Fleetwood Mac has accumulated near-mythological status, and the basic facts are straightforward enough: Mick Fleetwood heard the Buckingham Nicks album (1973, Polydor) while visiting Sound City studios in Los Angeles to scout equipment, was struck by Buckingham’s guitar work, called to offer him the vacant guitarist slot, and Buckingham negotiated his partner Nicks’s inclusion as a condition. What’s more analytically interesting is what the addition actually changed.

The Buckingham Nicks Album as Proof of Concept

The 1973 album, a commercial failure on release, is worth examining as a document of what the pair brought. Buckingham’s production — dense layered harmonics, treated guitars, meticulous attention to texture — was already fully formed. Nicks’s voice had the husky, slightly theatrical quality that would define her solo persona, and her lyrics were already drawn to imagery of witchcraft, fate, and romantic ruin. The album flopped largely because Polydor didn’t know how to market it, but the musicianship was not in dispute. Hearing it in the context of where Fleetwood Mac was in late 1974 makes Fleetwood’s decision look obvious in retrospect.

What Buckingham Added Beyond Guitar

The standard account emphasizes Buckingham as a guitarist replacement for Welch. This undersells him. Buckingham was primarily a record producer by sensibility, and his influence on the band’s studio approach was immediate and enormous. He pushed for more sophisticated arrangements, more adventurous use of studio time, and — eventually — the maximalist studio experimentation of Tusk. He was also the only member who had an overarching vision for what a given Fleetwood Mac album should sound like as an artifact, rather than a collection of individual songs. See Lindsey Buckingham — Production Vision and Creative Conflict and Sound and Production — From Blues to Soft Rock to Art-Pop.

The Romantic Complication

Buckingham and Nicks arrived as a couple and broke up during the recording of Rumours. This fact is so central to the album’s mythology that it risks overshadowing the musical analysis — see The Rumours Breakups — Relationships, Mythology, and What Actually Happened for an attempt to separate the two. What matters here is that the romantic entanglement was baked in from the start: the band did not recruit two independent musicians who happened to have chemistry; they recruited an established creative and romantic partnership whose dissolution would generate some of the most commercially successful breakup songs ever recorded.
