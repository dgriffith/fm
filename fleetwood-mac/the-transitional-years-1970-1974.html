My Notes

The Transitional Years 1970–1974

The years between Peter Green’s departure and the arrival of Lindsey Buckingham and Stevie Nicks are among the least studied in the band’s history, which is puzzling given how much happened. The band cycled through guitarists — Jeremy Spencer (who walked out mid-tour in 1971 to join the Children of God cult), Danny Kirwan (fired in 1972 after increasingly erratic behavior), Bob Welch, and Bob Weston — while simultaneously shifting its center of gravity from London to Los Angeles and from British blues to something more melodically open.

Christine McVie’s Emergence

Christine Perfect, who had been performing with the band informally since the Green era, married John McVie in 1968 and formally joined as a full member in 1970. Her presence changed the compositional chemistry immediately. Where Green and Kirwan had written from a blues idiom, Christine brought a Brill Building-influenced sensibility — economical melodies, clear emotional signposting, adult pop instincts that would eventually produce “Say You Love Me,” “Oh Daddy,” and “Everywhere.” She is arguably the most important figure in creating the conditions for the Rumours sound, a point frequently obscured by the Buckingham-Nicks narrative. See Christine McVie — The Underrated Architect.

Bob Welch and the American Pivot

Bob Welch (1971–1974) is the transitional figure who rarely gets his due. His tenure coincides with the band’s relocation to Los Angeles and a series of albums — Future Games, Bare Trees, Penguin, Mystery to Me, Heroes Are Hard to Find — that were more commercially oriented than the Green era without yet having the songwriting consistency of the Buckingham lineup. Welch’s own compositions had a slightly psychedelic, slightly moody character that anticipates certain strands of 1980s soft rock. His post-Mac solo career, particularly “Sentimental Lady” (a reworked Fleetwood Mac track) and “Ebony Eyes,” sold well but has not aged into critical respectability.

Welch’s departure in late 1974, citing exhaustion and disillusionment, opened the slot that Buckingham and Nicks would fill. The circumstances of the recruitment are covered in The Buckingham-Nicks Recruitment and What It Changed.

Why This Period Matters

The transitional period matters structurally because it explains two things: first, how the band survived the loss of its founding creative genius without dissolving (answer: Mick Fleetwood’s organizational tenacity and Christine McVie’s growing compositional weight); and second, why the band’s sound in 1975 sounded like Fleetwood Mac (the white album) rather than a straightforward continuation of any previous style. The Buckingham-Nicks addition was transformative partly because the band they joined was already in the middle of a sonic identity search.
