Fleetwood Mac — Origins and the Peter Green Era

Fleetwood Mac emerged in 1967 as a splinter from John Mayall’s Bluesbreakers, with Peter Green, Mick Fleetwood, and John McVie forming the nucleus. Green had replaced Eric Clapton in Mayall’s band and had quickly established himself as something different: where Clapton was pyrotechnic, Green was lyrical and restrained, with a vibrato and tone that B.B. King reportedly described as the only white guitarist who gave him the cold sweats. The addition of slide guitarist Jeremy Spencer and, later, Danny Kirwan gave the early band a three-guitar palette that was unusual even for the blues-drenched British scene.

The Sound

The Peter Green era albums — Peter Green’s Fleetwood Mac (1968), Mr. Wonderful (1968), and Then Play On (1969) — occupy a specific niche that separates them from the Clapton-Mayall school. Green’s compositions leaned heavily on minor keys, unresolved tension, and a restraint that bordered on melancholy. “The Green Manalishi (With the Two Prong Crown)” (1970) is perhaps the purest distillation: a grinding, almost liturgical riff that Green later claimed was inspired by a nightmare about a green dog representing money and demonic temptation. Peter Gabriel, Metallica, and Judas Priest have all covered it, each locating a different kind of menace in the original.

“Albatross” (1968) remains an outlier — an instrumental that reached number one in the UK and pointed toward the atmospheric, texture-first direction that would later characterize some of Buckingham’s production instincts — but its commercial success was almost accidental; the band did not pursue that direction immediately.

Green’s Decline

Green’s psychological deterioration beginning in 1969–1970 is one of the most documented and debated episodes in rock history. The precise causal sequence — LSD use, an extended stay in a German commune, possible early symptoms of what was later diagnosed as schizophrenia — remains contested. What is clear is that by early 1970 he was actively trying to give away his royalties and, in at least one account, threatened the band’s accountant with an air rifle when a royalty check arrived. He left the band in May 1970.

For decades the standard narrative framed Green’s breakdown as a cautionary tale about excess. The reassessment — ongoing since at least the mid-1990s — treats him primarily as a creative genius whose illness interrupted a trajectory that might otherwise have been talked about in the same breath as Hendrix. His late-career recordings with the Peter Green Splinter Group and the warm critical reception that greeted them supports this view. Green died in July 2020.

Legacy within the Band

The Peter Green era essentially has no direct musical continuity with the Rumours-era band beyond the name and the McVie-Fleetwood rhythm section. This discontinuity is important: it means Fleetwood Mac’s later success was not built on any foundation Green laid in terms of sound or songwriting approach. The connection is organizational and mythological. See also Lineup Instability as Structural Feature and Peter Green — Genius, Breakdown, and Reassessment.
