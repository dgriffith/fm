The Rumours Breakups — Relationships, Mythology, and What Actually Happened

The biographical scaffolding of Rumours — five people whose romantic relationships were collapsing simultaneously while they recorded an album together — is so dramatically compelling that it has become almost impossible to discuss the record without it. It is worth trying to establish what actually happened, what is mythology, and what the mythology is doing.

The Factual Record

Buckingham and Nicks ended their relationship during 1976, during pre-production and early recording. John and Christine McVie had decided to divorce, though they continued living together and working together throughout. Mick Fleetwood’s marriage to Jenny Boyd was under strain (they had separated and reconciled multiple times; the final divorce came in 1976). These facts are well-documented and uncontested.

What is sometimes overstated is the degree to which specific songs are autobiographical confessions rather than compositional constructions. “Go Your Own Way” is widely understood as Buckingham’s address to Nicks, and Nicks has confirmed this and expressed discomfort about having to sing backup on it. “Dreams” is often read as Nicks’s response — though Nicks has been more careful in interviews to describe it as a general meditation rather than a direct reply. “You Make Loving Fun” is Christine McVie’s song about an affair she was having with the band’s lighting director during the sessions — a detail that adds a layer of complication to the divorce narrative, since the song is explicitly about love rather than loss.

The Role of Mythology in the Reception

The biographical frame serves the album commercially and emotionally: it offers listeners a set of characters and a narrative arc that transforms a collection of pop songs into something that feels like a novel. This is not a criticism — the capacity to generate this kind of listener investment is a genuine artistic achievement. But it does mean that critical discussion of the album has been substantially colonized by the personal narrative at the expense of formal analysis.

The TikTok-era revival (“Dreams” re-charting in 2020) introduced the album to listeners who had no investment in the biographical context and responded purely to the music. This is, in a sense, the most interesting test the album has faced: it passed. See also Rumours — Making, Meaning, and Mythology.
