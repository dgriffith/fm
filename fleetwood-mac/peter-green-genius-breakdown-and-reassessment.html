Peter Green — Genius, Breakdown, and Reassessment

Peter Green occupies a peculiar position in rock historiography: universally praised by those who know his work, substantially unknown to the audience that knows the name Fleetwood Mac from Rumours. His story is one of the starkest illustrations of the costs of mental illness on a creative career, and the reassessment of his legacy — ongoing over the last three decades — raises genuine questions about what a “complete” career means when illness intervenes.

The Guitar Tone Question

Green’s tone is among the most discussed in British blues. He played a 1959 Les Paul Standard (serial number 9-1506) that he sold in 1970 to Gary Moore, who eventually sold it after Green asked for it back. The guitar has its own mythology — Moore recorded “Parisienne Walkways” on it; Green’s original recordings with it are treated as documents of a particular alchemical combination of instrument and player. The tone is described almost universally as having an “out-of-phase” quality produced by a reversed pickup magnet, though the precise configuration has been debated and replicated by luthiers ever since.

The Illness Narrative and Its Complications

The standard narrative presents Green’s LSD use as the precipitating cause of his breakdown. This is probably an oversimplification. Green himself in later interviews suggested that the drug experiences destabilized a pre-existing vulnerability, and the clinical picture — the grandiosity followed by withdrawal, the refusal of money, the paranoid episodes — is consistent with a serious mood or psychotic disorder that might have emerged regardless. The drugs may have accelerated a process rather than created it.

The ethical question that lingers is whether the people around Green — bandmates, management, label — could have intervened earlier or more effectively. The early 1970s had neither the clinical infrastructure nor the cultural vocabulary for the kind of crisis Green was experiencing, and the pace of the music industry offered no structural support for a musician who needed to slow down or stop.

Late Career and Rehabilitation

Green returned to recording in the 1990s with the Peter Green Splinter Group, producing several albums of blues standards and originals that demonstrated his musical instincts were intact even if his output was never as concentrated as the 1967–1970 peak. Critical reception was warm in a way that carefully acknowledged both the achievement and its limits. He died on July 25, 2020. The tributes from Buckingham, Nicks, and Mick Fleetwood were unanimous in treating him as the founding genius — an acknowledgment that the band’s famous lineup occupied a different creative territory that he had made possible but not defined.
